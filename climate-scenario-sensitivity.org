#+TITLE: The Main Cost Levers in Climate-Neutral Scenarios
#+OPTIONS: toc:nil

First Posted: 2026.10.27, [[https://github.com/nworbmot/nworbmot-blog][Last Revised]]: 2026.10.27, Author: [[https://www.nworbmot.org/][Tom Brown]]

What are the biggest levers for reducing costs in climate-neutral scenarios? Electrification, technological learning, biomass, CO_2 sequestration, grid expansion.

In our May 2026 paper [[https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2026.102464][A Minimal Methanol Backstop]] ([[./world-without-gas.html][blog post]], [[./methanol-faq.html][FAQ]]) we did sixteen sensitivity runs on a climate-neutral model for Europe. Changing the model assumptions shows how the total annualised system costs react. The first bar shows the absolute costs in the default scenario, with subsequent bars showing only differences. The size of each effect depends on the assumptions tested, and the percentage changes cannot simply be added together. It is of course just a model, not reality, but at least it's open source, so you're free to look inside and improve it.

**Electrification**: The premise of the methanol backstop was that high electrification helps to minimise the leftover sectors that need chemical fuel. If important sectors do not electrify as we assumed, then costs rise by 30% since the model is forced to use synthetic fuels in those sectors. Those sectors were high- and medium-temperature industry heat, domestic navigation, domestic aviation, as well as heavy-duty land transport. Note that the costs of the charging infrastructure and the electric vehicles themselves were not included in the calculation.

**Technological learning**: Europe aims to achieve net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050. Since most investments have to take place before then, and to avoid projecting too far into an uncertain future, we used cost assumptions for 2030. Most of the assumptions come from the [[https://ens.dk/en/analyses-and-statistics/technology-catalogues][Danish Energy Agency]]. If we use costs projected for 2050 instead, by which time technologies like solar and batteries have a further 20 years to reduce in cost thanks to [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experience_curve_effect][improved manufacturing]], then total costs drop by 13.3%.

**Biomass potentials**: We only allow the model to use biomass sources that don't cause additional land use, such as agricultural residues from food production like straw, forestry residues from wood harvesting, or biogenic waste. Dedicated energy crops are excluded. Our default case uses the "medium" assumptions from the European Commission's Joint Research Centre's [[https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esr.2019.100379][ENSPRESO dataset]], around 1550 TWh/a. Switching to the "high" potential of 3320 TWh/a reduces costs by 10% since the model uses biomethanol to replace all the e-methanol produced with captured CO_2. On the other hand, the "low" potential of 840 TWh/a raises costs by 8.1% as the model is forced to use direct air capture to source CO_2 for e-methanol instead of using biogenic carbon.

**CO_2 sequestration**: The default assumption in the model limits Europe-wide CO_2 sequestration to 200 MtCO_2/a. Sequestration in Europe is currently in the [[https://www.imperial.ac.uk/news/articles/engineering/earth-science/2026/global-underground-co-storage-hits-record-436-million-tonnes-as-london-register-expands-reach/][low single digits]], but the EU's Net Zero Industry Act has a target of 50 MtCO_2/a injection capacity by 2030, with projected storage needs of around 250 MtCO_2/a by 2040 ([[https://climate.ec.europa.eu/areas-action/industrial-carbon-management/eus-2030-carbon-storage-target_en][European Commission, 2026]]). It is [[https://concito.dk/en/udgivelser/e-fuels-or-storage-how-to-make-the-most-of-clean-electricity-and-captured-co2][often]] [[https://lnkd.in/p/d8jxqvSp][said]] that storing CO_2 underground should be cheaper than using it in e-fuels. On the other hand, adding electrolytic hydrogen to biogenic syngas with excess CO to produce e-biofuels can avoid some of the thermodynamic work of using raw CO_2 ([[https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12080][Zhang et al, 2026]]), and it's not clear that we can scale up all the CO_2 sequestration and pipeline infrastructure fast enough to compensate all hard-to-abate fossil CO_2 emissions. To explore the trade-offs, we raised the sequestration limit to 400 MtCO_2/a and then unlimited, where around 500 MtCO_2/a is sequestered. This reduces costs by 6.3% and 7.6% respectively, by allowing biogenic carbon dioxide removal (sequestering biogenic CO_2) to offset emissions from increased fossil-fuel use in aviation, shipping and the chemical industry, and thus lowering expensive electrolytic hydrogen production. If we simultaneously increase biomass potentials with unlimited sequestration, costs drop by 13.4% and the marginal abatement cost goes below 100 €/tCO_2 (453 MtCO_2/a is sequestered in this scenario).

**Grid expansion**: In the default scenario the model can choose the optimal electricity transmission corridors to reinforce, up to a total increase of the grid by 25% measured in MWkm. If we force the model to use today's grid, costs rise by 5.9%, since the grid can no longer balance wind fluctuations over long distances as effectively, and has to produce more backup fuels to be used locally. A thought experiment that removes power transmission altogether raises costs by 24%, showing the cost of foregoing electricity exchange between model regions.

**Relocation and imports of green products**: Allowing green steel and ammonia production to move from their current locations to the best renewable sites in Europe lowers costs by 1.3%. Allowing imports of green methanol, steel and ammonia from outside Europe reduces costs by 3.4%.

**Demand**: More obviously, changes to overall demand also have a big effect. Raising demand for road transport, aviation, shipping and high-value chemicals by 30% increases total costs by 15%.

* Methanol versus hydrogen/methane scenarios
  :PROPERTIES:
  :CUSTOM_ID: scenarios
  :END:

Our research paper ([[https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2026.102464][Glaum et al, 2026]]) explored using methanol as a
substitute for hydrogen and methane in backup electricity and heat
applications, thus removing the need for gaseous fuel networks. In the
"all networks scenario", both hydrogen and methane networks are
allowed. The original Figure 9 shows the differences between the
"minimal methanol backstop" and the "all networks" scenario for each
sensitivity in Panel A.
  
#+CAPTION: Minimal methanol backstop sensitivities, including difference to "all networks" in Panel A (Source: [[https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2026.102464][Glaum et al, 2026]])
[[./graphics/methanol_faq-260330/methanol-sensitivity.png]]

The cost premium for the minimal methanol backstop remains mostly in a fairly narrow 2-3% range, showing
that the use of the minimal methanol backstop is not that important
for the sensitivity analysis. Several sensitivities show larger differences, including "low electrification", "no power transmission" and "infinite sequestration".

  :PROPERTIES:
