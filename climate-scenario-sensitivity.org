#+TITLE: The Main Cost Levers for Climate Neutral Scenarios
#+OPTIONS: toc:nil

First Posted: 2026.10.27, [[https://github.com/nworbmot/nworbmot-blog][Last Revised]]: 2026.10.27, Author: [[https://www.nworbmot.org/][Tom Brown]]

What are the biggest levers for reducing costs in climate-neutral scenarios? Electrification, technological learning, biomass, CO_2 sequestration, grid expansion.

In our May 2026 paper [[https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2026.102464][A Minimal Methanol Backstop]] ([[./world-without-gas.html][blog post]] , [[./methanol-faq.html][FAQ]]) we did sixteen sensitivity runs on a climate-neutral model for Europe. Changing the model assumptions, you see how total costs react. It is of course just a model not reality, but at least it's open source, so you're free to look inside and improve it.

**Electrification**: The premise of the methanol backstop was that high electrification helps to minimise the leftover sectors that need chemical fuel. If important sectors do not electrify as planned, then costs rise by 30% since the model is forced to use synthetic fuels in those sectors. Those sectors were high- and medium-temperature industry heat, domestic navigation, domestic aviation, as well as heavy-duty land transport. Note that the costs of the charging infrastructure and the electric vehicles themselves were not included in the calculation. Note that the upfront cost of electric passenger cars is expected to be cheaper than their internal combustion counterparts in all vehicle classes by 2030 ([[https://theicct.org/cheaper-than-you-think-sooner-than-you-think-jul26/][ICCT 2026]]), and the total cost of ownership of trucks is predicted to favour electric vehicles by the same year 2030 ([[https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025][IEA 2025]]).

**Technological learning**: Europe aims to achieve net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050. Since most investments have to take place before then, and to avoid projecting too far into an uncertain future, we used cost assumptions for 2030. Most of the assumptions come from the [[https://ens.dk/en/analyses-and-statistics/technology-catalogues][Danish Energy Agency]]. If we use costs projected for 2050 instead, by which time technologies like solar and batteries have 20 further years to drop in cost thanks to [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experience_curve_effect][improved manufacturing]], then total costs drop by 13.3%.

**Biomass potentials**: We only allow the model to use sustainable biomass sources that don't cause additional land use, such as agricultural residues from food production like straw, forestry residues from wood harvesting, or biogenic waste. Our default case uses the "medium" assumptions from the European Commission's Joint Research Centre's [[https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esr.2019.100379][ENSPRESO dataset]], around 1550 TWh/a. Switching to the "high" potential of 3320 TWh/a reduces costs by 10% since the model uses biomethanol to replace all the e-methanol produced with captured CO_2. On the other hand, The "low" potential of 840 TWh/a raises costs by 8.1% as the model is forced to use direct air capture to source CO_2 for e-methanol instead of using biogenic carbon.

**CO_2 sequestration**: The default assumption in the model limits Europe-wide CO_2 sequestration to 200 MtCO_2/a. Sequestration in Europe is currently in the [[https://www.imperial.ac.uk/news/articles/engineering/earth-science/2026/global-underground-co-storage-hits-record-436-million-tonnes-as-london-register-expands-reach/][low single digits]], but the EU's Net Zero Industry Act has a target of 50 MtCO_2/a by 2030, with assumptions for 2050 of around 250 MtCO_2/a ([[https://climate.ec.europa.eu/areas-action/industrial-carbon-management/eus-2030-carbon-storage-target_en][European Commission, 2026]]). The CCS-friendly CATF included at most around 300 MtCO_2/a in their [[https://www.catf.us/resource/unlocking-europes-co2-storage-potential-analysis-optimal-co2-storage-europe/][scenario for Europe in 2050]]. It is [[https://concito.dk/en/udgivelser/e-fuels-or-storage-how-to-make-the-most-of-clean-electricity-and-captured-co2][often]] [[https://lnkd.in/p/d8jxqvSp][said]] that storing CO_2 underground should be cheaper than using it in e-fuels. On the other hand, adding electrolytic hydrogen to biogenic syngas with excess CO to produce e-biofuels can avoid some of the thermodynamic work of using raw CO_2 ([[https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12080][Zhang et al, 2026]]), and it's not clear that we can scale up all the CO_2 sequestration and pipeline infrastructure fast enough to compensate all hard-to-abate fossil CO_2 emissions. To explore the trade-offs, we raised the sequestration limit to 400 MtCO_2/a (above even CATF's scenario), and then unlimited, where around 500 MtCO_2 is sequestered. This reduces costs by 6.3% and 7.6% respectively, by allowing biogenic carbon dioxide removal (sequestering biogenic CO_2) to compensate increased fossil fuels in aviation, shipping and the chemical industry, and thus lowering expensive electrolytic hydrogen production. If we simultaneously increase biomass potentials, costs drop by 13.4% and the marginal abatement cost goes below 100 €/tCO_2.

**Grid expansion**: In the default scenario the model can choose the optimal electricity transmission corridors to reinforce, up to a total increase of the grid by 25% measured in MWkm. If we force the model to use today's grid, costs rise by 5.9%, since the grid can no longer balance wind fluctuations over long distances as effectively, and has to produce more backup fuels to be used locally. A thought experiment that removes power transmission altogether raises costs by 24%, showing the premium that has to be paid for regional autarky.

**Relocation/import green products**: Allowing green steel and ammonia production to move from their current locations to the best renewable sites in Europe lowers costs by 1.3%. Allowing imports of green methanol, steel and ammonia from outside Europe reduces further by 3.4%.

* Methanol versus hydrogen/methane scenarios
  :PROPERTIES:
  :CUSTOM_ID: scenarios
  :END:

Our research paper ([[https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2026.102464][Glaum et al, 2026]]) explored using methanol as a
substitute for hydrogen and methane in backup electricity and heat
applications, thus removing the need for gaseous fuel networks. In the
"all networks scenario", both hydrogen and methane networks are
allowed. The original Figure 9 shows the differences between the
"minimal methanol backstop" and the "all networks" scenario for each
sensitivity in Panel A.
  
#+CAPTION: Minimal methanol backstop sensitivities, including difference to "all networks" in Panel A (Source: [[https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2026.102464][Glaum et al, 2026]])
[[./graphics/methanol_faq-260330/methanol-sensitivity.png]]

The percentage change remains in a fairly narrow 2-3% range, showing
that the use of the minimal methanol backstop is not that important
for the sensitivity analysis. Only in extreme scenarios like "no power
transmission" and "infinite sequestration" do we see larger
differences.
